## What this theme covers

The aviation industry's commitment to net zero CO₂ emissions by 2050, formalised at the 41st ICAO Assembly in 2022 and reinforced through CORSIA, places measurable obligations on every link in the operational chain. Air traffic management's contribution is bounded but consequential: ATM cannot decarbonise the airframe or the fuel, but the way airspace is structured and flights are sequenced determines somewhere between 6% and 10% of the fuel burn on a typical European flight. ASW 2026 sessions in this track focus on the operational levers ANSPs control directly — continuous climb operations (CCO), continuous descent operations (CDO), free route airspace (FRA), trajectory optimisation through Trajectory Based Operations, and the reduction of Air Traffic Flow Management (ATFM) delays which translate directly into excess fuel burn from holding patterns and rerouting. The track also addresses sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) supply chain readiness from the airport-and-ANSP perspective, and the emerging methodology for accounting for non-CO₂ climate impacts including contrails and nitrogen oxides.

## Why it matters now

The European Union's ReFuelEU Aviation regulation requires a 2% SAF blend at EU airports from 2025, rising sharply through the decade, and CORSIA's first compliance phase (2024–2026) is now reporting verified emissions data. The ATM industry is being asked, for the first time, to produce auditable evidence that operational efficiency improvements are delivering the fuel savings ANSPs claim.

> EUROCONTROL's environmental assessment estimates that **inefficiencies in en-route airspace alone account for 8.6%–11.2% of avoidable CO₂ emissions** on European flights, equivalent to roughly 6–8 megatonnes of CO₂ per year at 2024 traffic levels [verify].

## Five questions, answered

### How does ATM contribute to aviation's net zero 2050 commitment?

ATM contributes to net zero through operational efficiency improvements that reduce fuel burn per flight. The principal mechanisms are continuous climb operations (allowing aircraft to climb to cruise altitude without intermediate level-offs), continuous descent operations (reducing the use of low-altitude vectoring before approach), free route airspace (allowing aircraft to fly the most efficient great-circle trajectory rather than following a fixed airway network), and Air Traffic Flow Management improvements that reduce holding patterns and tactical reroutes. EUROCONTROL has estimated that fully optimised European ATM operations could deliver fuel burn reductions of around 6%–10% relative to a 2019 baseline, with the larger share of the gain accruing in cruise and descent phases.

### What are continuous climb and continuous descent operations and why do they matter?

Continuous climb operations (CCO) allow a departing aircraft to climb from runway to cruise altitude without intermediate level-offs imposed by the controller for traffic separation. Continuous descent operations (CDO) allow an arriving aircraft to descend from cruise to final approach using minimal engine thrust, ideally remaining in idle thrust until close to the runway. Both procedures reduce fuel burn and noise relative to stepped climbs and conventional descents that rely on level segments. CCO and CDO are well established in low-density airspace, and the operational challenge in 2026 is extending them into busy terminal areas where traffic density traditionally required step-down procedures. Digital coordination tools and arrival management systems are the principal enablers.

### What is free route airspace and how widely is it implemented?

Free route airspace (FRA) is a flight planning and operational concept in which aircraft can flight-plan a direct route between defined entry and exit points within a designated airspace volume, rather than following a published airway network. The shorter trajectory reduces fuel burn and emissions. As of late 2024 FRA is operationally implemented across the great majority of European en-route airspace above flight level 305, with cross-border free route operations between adjacent ANSPs progressively replacing the older country-by-country FRA model. The remaining gaps are concentrated in lower airspace and in cross-border interfaces where FRA implementation is uneven between adjacent control centres.

### How are ATFM delays linked to environmental performance?

Air Traffic Flow Management (ATFM) delays are the holds applied to flights — typically before departure — when the available capacity in en-route or terminal airspace is insufficient to accommodate planned demand. Pre-departure ATFM delays are environmentally efficient because aircraft hold on the ground at idle. The environmental cost arises when capacity constraints cannot be resolved through pre-departure regulation and aircraft are instead rerouted, held airborne, or sequenced through extended approaches. EUROCONTROL's network performance data shows that en-route ATFM delay correlates with measurable excess fuel burn at the network level, which is why capacity recovery and resilience are environmental as well as operational priorities.

### Why is sustainable aviation fuel central to ATM conversations in 2026?

Sustainable aviation fuel is the principal in-sector lever for absolute CO₂ reduction in commercial aviation through 2050, but its rollout creates new operational and infrastructure questions for airports and ANSPs. The EU's ReFuelEU Aviation mandate requires a 2% SAF blend from 2025, rising to 6% by 2030 and 70% by 2050. Airport fuel infrastructure, refuelling logistics, and the implications of mixed SAF/conventional jet fuel for aircraft operations all touch operational planning. ANSPs are not directly responsible for SAF supply, but the network performance implications — including potential range and payload effects of higher SAF blends in some aircraft types — feed into the operational planning conversation that ASW 2026 hosts.
