## What this theme covers

The 2022–24 European summer seasons exposed structural fragility in the continent's airspace capacity. Traffic returned faster than ANSPs could rebuild controller workforces, the closure of Russian airspace and the Red Sea overflight constraints redirected flows onto already-loaded sectors, weather events triggered cascading delays, and industrial action in several Member States compounded the disruption.

ASW 2026 sessions in this track address what changed, what didn't, and what is now being built. The dominant threads are the post-summer capacity retrospectives ANSPs and the EUROCONTROL Network Manager have produced, and the formalisation into permanent mechanisms of the bilateral and multilateral cross-border contingency arrangements that kept traffic moving in 2024.

The track also covers the methodology of capacity declaration and forecasting under SES2+, and the practical rebuilding of resilience through controller recruitment, procedural simplification, and contingency procedures. Additional focus examines the airspace user perspective — how IATA member airlines have changed their assumptions about ATM capacity as a planning input.

## Why it matters now

The 2025 European summer was the first season since the post-pandemic recovery in which the Network Manager and the principal en-route ANSPs were able to publish capacity declarations that broadly held under operational stress. The 2026 challenge is different: maintaining the recovery while preparing for the structural workforce transitions through 2027–30 as the first cohorts of post-pandemic-trained controllers reach full operational status.

> EUROCONTROL reported that **average en-route ATFM delay per flight in summer 2024 reached 2.6 minutes**, more than double the SES Reference Period 3 target, with capacity-driven delays accounting for the largest share of the gap [verify].

## Five questions, answered

### What caused the European airspace capacity crisis of 2022–24?

The 2022–24 European capacity crisis was the result of multiple compounding factors rather than a single cause. Traffic recovered from the pandemic faster than ANSP workforces could be rebuilt, with controller training and certification cycles requiring three to five years for new entrants to reach unrestricted en-route ratings.

The closure of Russian airspace from 2022 redirected significant North Atlantic and Asia flows onto European core area sectors, particularly through the Maastricht and Karlsruhe Upper Area Control Centres. Industrial action in France, weather disruption, and the ongoing recovery of capacity in airspace transitioning to new flight data processing systems each contributed to the cumulative effect. The Network Manager's tactical mitigations could only partially offset the structural shortfall.

### How does Air Traffic Flow Management (ATFM) actually work?

ATFM is the function that balances airspace demand against available capacity by applying regulations — typically pre-departure delays — to flights forecast to enter capacity-constrained sectors. The European Network Manager, operating from EUROCONTROL's Brussels facility, monitors en-route and terminal capacity declarations from ANSPs and applies daily and tactical regulations to manage the flow. ATFM delays are reported per flight and per ANSP, with EUROCONTROL publishing daily, monthly, and annual network performance data. ATFM is not the same as airborne holding: a well-functioning ATFM regime keeps the holding on the ground at the departure airport, where it is fuel-efficient and minimises secondary disruption.

### What is cross-border contingency routing and why is it important?

Cross-border contingency routing is the operational practice of routing traffic through alternative ANSPs' airspace when the primary ANSP cannot accommodate demand because of unplanned capacity loss — most often industrial action, technical failure, or extreme weather. The 2022–24 crises forced the formalisation of bilateral and multilateral contingency agreements that previously operated as ad-hoc arrangements.

The most prominent examples are the multilateral arrangements coordinated through the Network Manager during French ATC strikes, in which adjacent ANSPs absorb traffic volumes that would otherwise have to be cancelled or routed at fuel-inefficient profiles. SES2+ and the revised Network Manager mandate provide the framework for these arrangements to operate as standing capabilities rather than emergency responses.

### How is airspace capacity actually measured and declared?

Capacity declaration is the process by which an ANSP states, in advance, the number of flights it expects each sector or sector group to be able to accept per hour over the planning horizon. Capacity is constrained by sector geometry, traffic complexity, controller staffing, technical system performance, and weather.

ANSPs publish capacity declarations to the Network Manager, which uses them to plan ATFM regulations and to coordinate cross-border flows. Under the SES performance scheme, capacity declarations and the actual capacity delivered are both audited, and divergences feed into the performance assessment that determines the cost recovery position of the ANSP. The methodology has tightened materially under SES2+ in response to the 2022–24 over-declaration controversies.

### What does network resilience mean in operational practice?

Network resilience is the capability of European airspace to absorb shocks — capacity loss, disruption events, weather, or geopolitical changes — without disproportionate operational consequences for airspace users.

In operational practice resilience comprises four measurable capabilities: contingency staffing and rapid sector reconfiguration within the affected ANSP; cross-border traffic absorption by adjacent ANSPs; tactical Network Manager regulation that prioritises essential flows; and structured communication with airspace users so that schedule decisions are taken on accurate information. ASW 2026 sessions address each of these dimensions and the metrics being developed under SES2+ to assess resilience as an auditable capability rather than as an aspirational descriptor.
