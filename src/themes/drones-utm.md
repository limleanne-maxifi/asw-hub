## What this theme covers

Unmanned traffic management (UTM) — the operational discipline that ensures unmanned aircraft can operate safely in shared airspace — has moved from regulatory concept to operational rollout. The European Union's U-space regulation, formally Implementing Regulation (EU) 2021/664 supported by 2021/665 and 2021/666, entered application in January 2023 and is being progressively activated by Member States designating U-space airspace volumes.

ASW 2026 sessions in this track address the operational state of U-space across the EU; the certification and supervision of U-space service providers (USSPs); the architecture of the UTM/ATM interface, including the obligations on ANSPs to coordinate with USSPs in shared airspace; the development of drone corridor governance for routine BVLOS operations; and the European reference architecture work emerging from D-FLIGHT and equivalent national programmes.

The track also examines counter-UAS — the operational and regulatory response to unauthorised drone incursions in controlled airspace — and the integration questions raised by larger uncrewed platforms including high-altitude operations and inner-space platforms.

## Why it matters now

The first U-space airspace designations went operational in 2024–25 in the Netherlands, Italy, and France. By 2026 multiple EU Member States have certified USSPs in active operational service, and the first national-scale beyond-visual-line-of-sight (BVLOS) operations are running in routine commercial use rather than under one-off authorisations. The framework is no longer aspirational.

## Five questions, answered

### What is U-space and how does it differ from controlled airspace?

U-space is the EU's regulatory and operational framework for managing unmanned aircraft operations in defined airspace volumes through digital, automated services rather than through traditional ATC voice communication. U-space airspace is established by Member States as a discrete designation in which uncrewed aircraft must access mandatory U-space services — including network identification, geo-awareness, flight authorisation, and traffic information — provided by certified U-space service providers.

U-space differs from controlled airspace in that the operational management is delivered digitally, by USSPs supervising drone operations through their service interfaces, rather than by air traffic controllers issuing voice clearances. Manned aviation operating in U-space airspace must broadcast electronic conspicuity to be visible to USSP services, but receives traffic information rather than separation services from the U-space framework.

### Who are U-space service providers (USSPs) and what do they do?

A U-space service provider is a certified entity authorised by the relevant Member State competent authority to deliver U-space services to drone operators within designated U-space airspace. The mandatory U-space services under EU 2021/664 are network identification (continuous identification and tracking of drone operations), geo-awareness (provision of dynamic information on airspace constraints and restrictions), flight authorisation (deconfliction of planned drone operations against other approved flights), and traffic information.

USSPs operate under EASA-aligned certification and supervision regimes, and provide digital interfaces to drone operators that automate flight planning, authorisation, and in-flight situational awareness. Multiple USSPs may compete within the same U-space volume.

### How do U-space and conventional ATM coordinate at the interface?

The UTM/ATM interface is the operational and information exchange between USSPs and the air navigation service provider responsible for the surrounding controlled airspace. Coordination occurs at the boundary of U-space airspace, where uncrewed flights may transit into or out of ATC-managed airspace, and within shared airspace where manned and unmanned operations coexist.

The interface is specified in part through SES-aligned procedures and SWIM data services, with the ANSP retaining responsibility for separation in conventional controlled airspace and for the integration of drone operations that require ATC clearance. The 2026 operational state is uneven across Member States: where the ANSP and the USSP have operational integration agreements, the interface functions smoothly; where they do not, friction is significant.

### What is BVLOS and how is it scaling in 2026?

BVLOS — beyond visual line of sight — describes drone operations in which the operator cannot maintain unaided visual contact with the aircraft, which is the operational mode required for any commercially scalable drone use including delivery, infrastructure inspection, surveying, and emergency response. BVLOS operations require either Specific Category authorisation under the EU drone regulation or operation within the operational risk envelope of an established Standard Scenario.

By 2026 multiple Member States have approved standard scenarios that enable routine BVLOS operations within defined geographic and operational parameters. Several national programmes — including the UK Civil Aviation Authority's BVLOS pathway and Italy's drone corridor designations — have moved BVLOS from one-off trials into structured commercial deployment.

### What is D-FLIGHT and why is it referenced as a UTM architecture?

D-FLIGHT is the Italian reference UTM architecture, developed by ENAV (the Italian air navigation service provider) and partner organisations, that has provided one of the more developed national implementations of U-space service infrastructure. D-FLIGHT is referenced in European policy and operational discussions as an example of how the EU U-space regulation translates into a working national service architecture, including network identification, geo-awareness, and flight authorisation services for drone operations.

Other Member States have developed equivalent national reference architectures — including programmes in France, Germany, the Netherlands, and Spain — and ASW 2026 sessions compare the architectural and operational choices being made across the EU as U-space implementation progresses.
